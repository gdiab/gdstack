# Story development

Use this short workflow when the requested deliverable is a nonfiction story, post, or essay drawn from real experience or evidence. Skip it for routine line edits, summaries, factual documentation, and detect-only requests. If the author has already supplied the motivation, angle, and examples, use them without repeating an interview.

## Find the author's reason for telling it

Read the available source material and the author's current direction. Distinguish recorded events from the author's assessment of them. Use an applicable voice profile or writing sample as guidance, not a list of mannerisms to reproduce.

If the human stakes are missing, ask one focused question, for example: "What about this experience stuck with you: something surprising, frustrating, satisfying, or a moment when your opinion changed? Rough words are useful." A relevant alternative is what they wanted and why previous attempts fell short. Ask for what the records cannot tell you; continue independent evidence work while awaiting a reply when possible. Do not invent first-person memories, motives, feelings, or a turning point to fill the gap.

## Choose a thread from the evidence

Briefly state the central question or tension the material can support. Follow the author's supplied angle; propose one when it is missing. This is a drafting decision, not an automatic approval checkpoint.

A useful thread connects what the author wanted, what they tried, what they learned, and what remains unresolved. Use only the parts that fit the experience. Do not force a conversion story, dramatic reversal, success arc, fixed number of scenes, or universal lesson.

Select incidents because they explain a decision or consequence for the reader. For each technical example, connect the observation to what someone chose and what that changed. Prefer a concrete failure, correction, or behavior over a catalogue of tools and milestones. Include a limitation or contrary result when leaving it out would distort the story.

## Keep the record; select the story

Preserve the full source material separately from the narrative. Link to evidence where useful. When revising an existing file substantially, retain a recoverable previous version through the project's normal history or an appropriate snapshot. Do not overwrite the source record with its selective retelling.

Story development permits omitting nonessential facts or moving them into supporting notes, captions, callouts, or a disclosure. In this mode, the core's claim-preservation checks mean preserving the meaning and support of selected claims, including limitations that materially qualify them. They do not require every fact in the source record to appear in the article. Ordinary humanizing retains the original keep-every-supported-claim rule.

Keep measurements, observed outcomes, reported claims, and the author's interpretation distinguishable. Qualify a causal claim when the evidence only supports an impression. Detailed accounting belongs in the main narrative only when it advances the story; otherwise retain enough context for the reader to interpret it and put the calculation alongside it.

## Preserve the person's response

Use the author's supplied language for motives and judgments. Keep uncertainty when it describes a real unknown, mixed feelings when both sides matter, and a genuine open question when it drives the experiment. Do not delete them merely to make the prose sound decisive.

Let humor grow from an observed detail or the author's reaction. Do not require a joke, recycle a signature phrase, or add a confession to make the author seem relatable. Read individual style patterns in context rather than flattening every paragraph into the same approved rhythm.

The ending may remain provisional. Return to what the author now understands, intends to try, or still needs to establish. Do not manufacture a victory or an industry-wide conclusion.

## Review the reader's experience

For a longer post whose structure benefits from images or supporting detail, review it in the intended reading format using the project's existing tools and design. Use screenshots, captions, and compact callouts where they explain something; remove decorative diagrams and avoid turning the article into a dashboard. Do not make an HTML artifact a requirement for a short text-only task.

Keep private evidence out of public deliverables. Label fixtures, prototypes, and runtime captures accurately. Visual polish does not establish a product result.

Apply the Humanizer prose pass after the structural work. Use eval.md with the preservation interpretation above. For this mode, return the requested draft and only the editorial explanation the user needs, rather than two complete rewrites. Detect-only requests still receive findings without edits.

Before calling the draft ready, check:

- Does the story reflect the author's supplied motivation and assessment?
- Does each chosen incident explain a decision or consequence, with its supporting evidence retained?
- Are factual claims and causal language supported, including material limitations?
- Did uncertainty, mixed feelings, and specific personality survive without invented experience?
- Can a reader unfamiliar with the project follow it, and does the presentation help them do so?
